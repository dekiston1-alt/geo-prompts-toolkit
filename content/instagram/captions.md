## Day 5 — Educational: How to Start Growing Herbs at Home (Even If You've Failed Before)

You are not bad at growing plants. You were probably growing the wrong ones.

Most people start with basil or mint from the grocery store — already stressed, already root-bound, already half-dead. They fail, and they decide they don't have a green thumb.

Medicinal herbs are different. Most of them are wild plants with thousands of years of surviving without human help. They want to grow. Your job is just to not get in the way.

Here's how I tell beginners to start:

Start with seeds, not transplants. You understand the plant better when you watch it emerge from nothing.

Pick three herbs max. Lemon balm, calendula, and chamomile are my top three for beginners — forgiving, fast, and useful.

Use quality soil. This is not where to cut corners. Good soil does half the work.

Water consistently but don't overwater. Most medicinal herbs prefer slightly dry over soggy.

That's it. Honestly.

## Day 8 — Educational: The Difference Between Culinary and Medicinal Herbs

Here's something most people don't realize: the basil on your pizza and the holy basil in your supplement are not the same plant.

Most culinary herbs have some medicinal value — but medicinal herbs are specifically chosen for their therapeutic compounds, and they're often varieties that taste too intense or too bitter to eat in large quantities.

The medicinal version of familiar plants tends to have higher concentrations of the active ingredients: more rosmarinic acid in medicinal rosemary, more apigenin in chamomile bred for medicine rather than decoration, more adaptogens in tulsi compared to regular basil.

This is why the seed kit matters. You're not just growing "herbs" — you're growing specific medicinal varieties selected for potency and purpose.

## Day 11 — Behind the Scenes: How I Dry and Store My Herbs

Harvest day is one of my favorite days. Here's how I do it.

I cut in the morning, after the dew dries but before the afternoon heat pulls the oils out of the leaves. This is when medicinal herbs are at their most potent — the volatile compounds are concentrated right before the sun peaks.

I gather loosely. I never take more than a third of any plant at once — this keeps the plant productive all season.

I bundle small amounts together with twine and hang them upside down in my kitchen. Good airflow, low humidity, indirect light. Most herbs are fully dry in 7-14 days.

Once dry, they go into labeled glass jars — airtight, kept out of direct light. Properly stored, most dried medicinal herbs retain potency for 1-2 years.

Then I use them. Teas, tinctures, salves, bath soaks, steam inhalations. The garden produces, and I don't let anything go to waste.

This whole process — from seed to harvest to use — is what makes herbal medicine feel real and mine. It's not supplementing. It's participating.
