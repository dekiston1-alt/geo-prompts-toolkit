## Day 4

**Subject:** Why your supplements might be failing you
**Preview text:** It's not you. It's what's actually in the bottle.

---

Hi,

I want to share something today that I think is genuinely important — and that the supplement industry would really prefer you didn't think too hard about.

Let me start with a question: do you know what's in your supplements beyond the "active" ingredients?

If you go to your cabinet right now and flip one of your bottles over, you'll likely find a section called "Other Ingredients." Depending on the brand, you might see things like:

- Magnesium stearate (a lubricant used in manufacturing)
- Silicon dioxide (a flow agent)
- Microcrystalline cellulose (a filler derived from wood pulp)
- Titanium dioxide (a whitening agent that some research has raised concerns about)
- Artificial colorings or coatings

These are called excipients, and while they're considered safe in small doses, they're not doing anything for your health. They're in there for the manufacturer's convenience, not yours.

But the filler problem is actually secondary to a bigger issue: bioavailability.

Bioavailability refers to how much of a substance your body can actually absorb and use. And this is where synthetic supplements often fall short. The synthetic form of vitamin E is less bioavailable than the natural form. Synthetic folate is metabolized differently than food-derived folate. Isolated compounds — pulled out of their natural plant matrix and crystallized into a capsule — don't always behave the same way in your body as the whole plant does.

This isn't fringe science. It's basic biochemistry. And it has real implications for whether the products you're spending money on are actually doing what you hope they're doing.

Now, I'm not saying supplements are useless. There are absolutely times, conditions, and nutrients where supplementation is important and effective. But for many of the common herbal supplements people take for stress, sleep, immunity, and skin — the plant itself, grown and used properly, is often the better option.

When you brew a cup of chamomile tea from flowers you grew yourself, you're getting the whole plant — flavonoids, terpenoids, essential oils, all of the compounds working together the way they evolved to work. That's different from a standardized extract in a capsule. And in many cases, it's more effective.

Growing your own medicine isn't anti-science. It's actually more aligned with how these plants were studied and used throughout the history of herbal medicine.

I'm not asking you to throw away your supplements. I'm inviting you to consider what it would mean to grow even a few of the herbs you rely on most.
